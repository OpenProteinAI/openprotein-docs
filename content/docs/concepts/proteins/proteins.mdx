## What is a protein?
A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a three-dimensional shape. That shape determines what the protein does: catalysing a reaction, binding a target, carrying a signal or giving a cell its structure.

Every protein is built from the same 20 standard amino acids. The order of those amino acids is the protein's *sequence*, and the sequence is what the cell's machinery reads from DNA. Change the sequence and you can change the shape, and with it the protein's behaviour.

## Sequence, structure and function
Biologists describe a protein at four levels:

| Level | What it describes |
| :---- | :---- |
| Primary structure | The amino acid sequence, read from the N-terminus to the C-terminus |
| Secondary structure | Local folding patterns such as alpha helices and beta sheets |
| Tertiary structure | The full 3D fold of one chain |
| Quaternary structure | How several chains assemble into one complex, such as an antibody's heavy and light chains |

The sequence encodes everything above it. This is why models that only read sequences can still learn a great deal about structure and function, and why structure prediction models can fold a protein from its sequence alone.

## Protein fitness
**Fitness** is a quantitative measure of how well a protein variant performs a selected function. Each sequence has a measurable phenotype, such as catalytic efficiency, binding affinity, thermodynamic stability, expression titre or a weighted combination of these. Mapped across every possible sequence, these values form the fitness landscape.

The effect of a mutation is usually reported relative to a reference sequence, often the wild type, on a log scale so that effects are comparable across assays.

Most random mutations lower fitness, because evolution has already tuned natural proteins to work. Protein engineering looks for the rare mutations that improve it. OpenProtein.AI helps in two ways:

* Foundation models such as PoET-2 learn from millions of natural sequences which mutations evolution tends to accept, and score new sequences without any lab data.
* Property predictors learn from your own assay data to predict the specific property you measured.

## Proteins on OpenProtein.AI
The platform works with protein sequences directly, and with structures where a model needs them.

* **Single-chain proteins** are plain sequences, entered as text, FASTA or CSV.
* **Multichain proteins**, such as an antibody heavy and light chain pair, are written as one string with a `:` between chains. In the Python API you can also build them as a [Complex](/references/python-api/molecules#openprotein.molecules.Complex).
* **Structures** are read from PDB or mmCIF files, or predicted from sequence with the structure prediction tools.
