After training, models (such as [property predictors](/concepts/models/property-predictors)) output a few useful metrics: cross-validation, which measures 
prediction accuracy, calibration, which measures whether the mode's uncertainty estimates
are reliable, and a training curve that shows whether training has converged. Users should
review all three before using a model. 

## Cross-validation 

Cross-validation is used to evaluate how well the model will generalize to unseen data. 
In practice, this means splitting your data into k folds, training on all but one fold, 
then predicting on the held-out fold. By default, our platform runs 5-fold cross-validation. 

Two correlation metrics are reported: **Spearman's rho**, which measures rank correlation, 
and **Pearson's r**, which measures linear association. Use **Spearman's rho** if your data is ordinal, skewed, contains outliers, or follows a non-linear but strictly 
increasing or decreasing trend. Otherwise, if your data follows a normal distribution, use 
Pearson's r. Correlation values range from -1 to 1. 1 indicates perfect positive correlation, 
while -1 indicates eprfect negative correlation and 0 indicates no correlation.

### Troubleshooting 

Several factors affect your model's cross-validation performance, which may result 
in lower-than-desired correlation. 
- Measurement noise is a fundamental limitation, so even a perfect model will not 
have a correlation of 1.0. Working with limited data also introduces uncertainty into 
the sequence-function relationship that limits the predictive power of the model. 
- Our models are uncertainty-aware: they output a standard deviation indicating 
confidence in the prediction. More data will generally lead to a better model with 
higher confidences, but a model with high uncertainty can still guide designs 
towards sequence variants that are most likely to have the best properties. In 
this case, more sequences likely need to be tested because the sequence-function 
library isn't fully understood. This will also be reflected in the [success 
probabilities of variants and libraries](/concepts/design/bayesian-optimization). 

## Model calibration 

Calibration measures whether a model's uncertainty estimates are reliable. This means
that the predictive intervals it outputs should contain the true property value 
with the stated probability: a reported 70% credible interval should contain the true value 70%
of the time. 

The calibration plot visualizes this by comparing predicted probability to observed
frequency. The diagonal line represents perfect calibration. To quantify this, we
compute and reported the **Expected Calibration Error (ECE)**: the weighted average 
of the absolute difference between predicted coverage and observed frequency across 
bins. Lower ECE values indicate better calibration. 

Calibration matters because our downstream design algorithms account for uncertainty 
when proposing new libraries.

## Training curves 

Training curves display how the model's loss changes during the training process. Lower values
indicate better performance. In general, we should expect the loss curve to decrease quickly at the 
start, and then flatten as the model performance plateaus. A curve that does not flatten suggests 
underfitting, while a rising curve could suggest overfitting.
