A property predictor is a model that predicts a property of a protein from its sequence or structure. These can be pre-trained predictors, or predictors trained on your data. 
On our platform, we use a particular type of model, Gaussian Process models, for training specialized property predictors on your assay data. 

## Gaussian process

Our platform trains specialized property predictors on your assay data using Gaussian Process (GP) models. 

Formally, a Gaussian Process is a collection of random values, such that any finite subset of them follows a multivariate normal distribution. A GP is specified by a mean function,
which gives the expected value of the function at each input, and a covariance function, $k(x, x')$, also called the kernel, which defines how correlated the function values are 
at two different inputs. Here, $x$ is the embedding vector of a protein sequence given by a foundation model. 

A few properties of GPs make them well-suited to protein engineering. 
- **GPs predict distributions, not point values**: GPs predict both mean and uncertainty. This is important for our downstream Bayesian optimization algorithms so that we can trade-off on *exploitation* and *exploration*.
- **GPs work well with small datasets**: GPs are designed to leverage prior knowledge through their covariance kernel.
- **GPs naturally handle new data without retraining**: GPs 'add new data' simply by conditioning on them. 

### Covariance kernels

The covariance kernel describes how similarity in the embedding space translates into correlation in the property space. 

An an example, the default kernel (and a popular one) is the **radial basis function (RBF)** kernel, also called the squared exponential or Gaussian kernel. This kernel expresses the 
assumption that sequences whose embeddings are close together have correlated property values. This correlation then decays with embedding distance. The RBF kernel has two hyperparameters, 
both fitted during model training: the lengthscale, which sets how far two embedding vectors must be before their property values are effectively independent, and sigma, which sets the scale of 
variation of the property. 

Other kernels encode different assumptions. We support several other standard kernels, including the Linear, Matérn and Rational quadratic kernels. To learn more about different kernels, we recommend taking a look at scikit-learn’s 
[documentation on Gaussian Process kernels](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/gaussian_process.html#gp-kernels). We encourage users to experiment with different kernels
when training their models to find the one that best suits their application 

### Mean and uncertainty

For each sequence, a GP property predictor returns both the posterior mean and variance of the property. This is crucial for the **Design** step of our multi-objective design algorithm, 
which uses this information to balance proposing sequences that the model thinks will maximize your design objective, with sequences in unknown regions that the model has not yet 
explored. This also provides us with a principled framework to evaluate the probability of success of a given library.
