Bayesian optimization is a model-based strategy for optimizing black-box functions. On 
our platform, Bayesian optimization is used in the **Design** step of the DBTL loop to search 
sequence space for variants that maximize the probability of achieving a set of quantitative objectives. 

## What is Bayesian optimization? 

Bayesian optimization uses probabilistic models to choose which candidates to evaluate next 
by explicitly considering uncertainty in the prediction. This is particularly useful 
for protein engineering, where the space of possible variants is astronomically large, 
but each round of assay measurements is slow and expensive. 

When training on measurement data, our platform uses Bayesian property predictors that
output both a mean and an uncertainty for a given prediction. This allows the design algorithm 
to calculate the probability that a candidate sequence meets a target, rather than just its predicted value. 

Because predictions are distributions rather than point values, we can balance search between two behaviors: 
- **Exploitation**: Sequences that are similar to high-performing variants in a dataset have confident predictions
near the target, giving them a high probability of success
- **Exploration**: Sequences in less-explored regions have uncertain predictions, and may still be 
promising to explore. 

## Design objectives and criteria 

### Objectives and criteria 

To perform Bayesian Optimization, we must first define one or more **objectives**, which can 
thought of as design goals. Each objective is built from one or more **criteria**. 

A **criterion** specifies a proeprty, a direction, and a target value: for example,
binding affinity (property) > (direction) 1 (target value). This means that the solver
will look for sequences that satisfy every criterion in that objective at once. One 
useful example of this is combining several models of the same proeprty within one
objective, which effectively ensembles them. 

In contrast, **objectives** are traded off against each other. This means that the
solver will search for solutions across the Pareto front defined by the set of 
objectives. 

In addition to custom objectives, users may also choose to include a special 
**number of mutations criteria** that adds the number of mutations as its own 
objective. The solver will then explore trade-offs between mutational load and
the other targets. 

### Pareto front 

In multi-objective optimization, there may be no single best-performing sequence 
because improving one sequence could come at the cost of another. The **Pareto front** 
is defined as the set of **Pareto-optimal** solutions that optimally trade off
between those objectives. A solution is **Pareto-optimal** if no other solution 
dominates it, that is, no other solution is at least as good as that solution on 
every objective and also strictly better on at least one. 

When multiple objectives are specified, our design algorithms searching along the 
Pareto front to return solutions that trade off between these objectives in 
different proportions. 

## Predicted library performance 

The **Predicted Library Performance** summarizes the expected performance of sequences
within a design panel, and is automaically calculated as part of **Optimize**. Note
that these metrics will update as you apply filters or generate sequences. These metrics
are intended to help users to make informed decisions about the composition and size 
of the final library that they intend to build and test. 

### Successes 

The **Successes** tab illustrates the probability distribution over the number of
successes, i.e. the number of sequences predicted to meet the specified design criteria. The
plot reports the mean, standard deviation and 95% confidence interval (95% CI) over the expected
number of successes. 

Note that the number of expected successes should be considered in relation to library size.
